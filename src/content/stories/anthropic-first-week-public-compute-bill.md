Anthropic priced its IPO at $83 per share on 18 November, above an initial range of $70 to $78, and sold 980 million shares, up from the 825 million initially indicated. The offering raised about $81 billion and valued the company at about $1.5 trillion. Shares began trading on Nasdaq under the ticker ANT on 19 November, opening at $111.50, about 34% above the IPO price.

## The debut was the easy part

The stock closed its first day at $97.17, about 17% above the IPO price, then fell about 9% in early trading the following day. At its debut the offering produced a valuation of about $2 trillion, ahead of SpaceX's June listing.

Two valuations, two moments. The $1.5 trillion figure belongs to the pricing on 18 November. The $2 trillion figure belongs to the first trade. The distance between them is the debut, and it is the least informative number in the quarter.

## Compute took 58.2% of revenue

Per its prospectus and reporting on it, compute and infrastructure expense reached 58.2% of revenue in the third quarter of 2026, against 48.1% in the preceding quarter. In dollars, $9,863 million of expense against $16,956 million of revenue, compared with $5,558 million against $11,555 million in the second quarter.

Revenue grew about 47% between the two quarters. Compute and infrastructure expense grew about 77%. That is the whole story in two numbers: the larger line grew faster than the smaller one, and the gap between them narrowed.

Put another way, about 80% of the increase in revenue went to compute and infrastructure. Every extra dollar of sales carried roughly eighty cents of additional expense behind it, before staff, sales or research.

## $5.0 billion bought at a premium

Anthropic bought $5.0 billion of short-term compute in the third quarter. The company says those terms currently cost significantly more than multiyear capacity.

Short-term capacity buys flexibility: it can be switched off, moved, or repriced when a model changes shape. Multi-year capacity buys a lower rate and a longer commitment. Paying a premium for the short version is what a company does when it does not yet know what it will need, and the premium lands in the quarter rather than being spread over years.

## A profit became a loss

The operating result moved from a $974 million profit in the second quarter to a $567 million loss in the third. The nine-month operating loss was $1,260 million.

The swing is smaller than the rise in compute and infrastructure expense between the two quarters, so lines other than compute must have moved the other way. The compute line is still the one the prospectus makes visible quarter by quarter, and the one that scales with usage.

Separately, $19,240 million of training compute sat in research and development. That figure does not pass through the 58.2%, so the expense ratio does not include it.

## $900.9 billion committed, ordered a year ahead

The prospectus puts committed compute and lease spending at about $900.9 billion, including $451.6 billion expected before 31 December 2030. Compute is ordered 12 to 18 months ahead under take-or-pay provisions.

Take-or-pay means the bill arrives whether or not the capacity is used. Ordering that far ahead is a bet on demand, made at a point when the company's quarterly revenue was $16,956 million. The $451.6 billion scheduled before the end of the decade is more than 26 times that quarter's revenue.

That commitment is the part of the prospectus a supplier would read first, and the part an investor should read second.

## The margin is the number to watch

A first week of trading measures appetite, not unit economics. The 58.2% measures unit economics, and it moved ten percentage points in one quarter, in the wrong direction.

One of the reports behind this story, from CTOL Digital on 23 November, paired the 58.2% compute share with a price cut on OpenAI's GPT-6.2 Astra API. If API prices fall while compute commitments are fixed 12 to 18 months out, the ratio has only one way to move unless volume absorbs it.

The next set of quarterly figures will show whether the third quarter was a step change or the start of a trend. Watch the compute share first, the short-term premium second, and the $451.6 billion schedule last. Only one of those three is under the company's control.
